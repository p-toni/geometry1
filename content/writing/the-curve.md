## 1958

In 1949, a New Zealander named Bill Phillips built a model of the British economy out of perspex tanks and coloured water. Pumps, valves, floats. Money was a red-dyed liquid; you could open a tap marked *taxation* and watch the level drop somewhere else. He built it as a student, with parts he could afford, and it worked well enough that universities bought copies.

He had come to economics the long way. He left school early, hunted crocodiles in Australia, ran a cinema, and studied electrical engineering in London. He was in Singapore when it fell, escaped to Java, and spent three and a half years in a Japanese prison camp, where he built a wireless out of scavenged parts and kept it hidden. He was appointed MBE for it in 1946. He read sociology, found it did not answer his questions, and switched.

In November 1958 he published *The Relation Between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861–1957.*

Read the title slowly, because the thing it says is not the thing everyone remembers. Not prices. **Money wages.** As unemployment fell, wages rose faster; as unemployment rose, wages slowed. Ninety-six years of British data, and the relation held through the gold standard, two wars, a depression and a welfare state.

And it was a real fit, not a line drawn through everything. He fitted the curve to **1861–1913** and then showed that the decades he had not used — through 1948, and then 1948 to 1957 — fell close to it. The curve predicted data it had never seen.

That is as good as an empirical regularity in economics gets. It is compression in the strict sense: ninety-six years of a country's wage history, held in one line.

## 1960

Two years later Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow took it to the American Economic Association.

Their paper is called *Analytical Aspects of Anti-Inflation Policy*, and they did two things to Phillips's curve. They swapped his money wages for **price inflation**, which is the version everyone now pictures. And they redrew it for the United States, from the previous twenty-five years of American data.

The caption under their figure, on page 192, is the sentence that changed what the curve was for:

> the menu of choice between different degrees of unemployment and price stability

A menu. Not a description of what had happened — a list of what you could order. Two percent more unemployment buys you this much less inflation; choose.

Now the part I did not know until I went and read it, and which turns this from a story about hubris into something worse.

**They printed the warning on the menu.** On the same pages:

> It would be wrong, though, to think that our menu that relates obtainable price and unemployment behavior will maintain its same shape in the longer run.

They said the curve would move. They said a sustained low-pressure economy would act on wage expectations and shift it downward, and that a long low-pressure period could build structural unemployment and shift it up. It is all there, in the text, adjacent to the word *menu*.

The menu travelled. The warning did not.

I want to be careful here, because the story where 1960s economists naively believed in a permanent trade-off is a retrospective construction, and historians of the field have spent decades arguing about it. What is not in dispute is the transmission: the figure and its caption became policy furniture, and the two paragraphs qualifying them did not.

**A caveat attached to a usable object is not a caveat. It is decoration on a lever.**

## December 1967

The refutation arrived before the failure did, which is the detail I keep turning over.

In August 1967 Edmund Phelps published *Phillips Curves, Expectations of Inflation and Optimal Unemployment over Time.* On 29 December 1967, Milton Friedman gave the presidential address to the American Economic Association in Washington and said the same thing from a different direction.

Friedman is generous about Phillips and then precise about the defect:

> Phillips' analysis of the relation between unemployment and wage change is deservedly celebrated as an important and original contribution. But, unfortunately, it contains a basic defect — the failure to distinguish between nominal wages and real wages.

And then the claim the whole essay turns on:

> there is always a temporary trade-off between inflation and unemployment; there is no permanent trade-off. The temporary trade-off comes not from inflation per se, but from unanticipated inflation

*Unanticipated.* The curve worked because, across those ninety-six years, nobody was systematically trying to work it. Wage bargainers were not pricing in a central bank's intention to run the economy hot. The moment they do, the relation the curve described is not the relation you are operating in.

Friedman's prediction was specific: hold unemployment below its natural rate and you will need not inflation but *accelerating* inflation to keep it there.

Phelps published in 1967. Friedman spoke in 1967. The thing they described did not visibly happen until 1974.

## July 1975

At a Reserve Bank of Australia conference in Sydney, Charles Goodhart put a line in a conference paper about British monetary management. He has since said it was a throwaway, a humorous aside, not based on any deeper analysis:

> Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

He was describing something he had watched happen. In September 1971 the Bank of England's *Competition and Credit Control* removed direct lending controls; bank intermediation surged and broad money growth passed **25 per cent** in 1972–73, and the stable money-demand relationship the authorities had been steering by came apart. Later, under £M3 targeting from 1979, money growth overshot the targets by roughly **100 per cent** in the first two and a half years. The aggregate stopped corresponding to the thing it had measured, partly because banks reorganised themselves around the target.

Meanwhile the American numbers had done what Friedman said they would. In 1970, unemployment was 4.9 per cent and inflation 5.8. By 1975, unemployment was **8.5** and inflation **9.1**.

There is no downward-sloping curve that accommodates that pair. Both went up.

## 1980

Robert Lucas published the general case in 1976, in a conference volume titled, with no irony available to it, *The Phillips Curve and Labor Markets*:

> Given that the structure of an econometric model consists of optimal decision rules for economic agents, and that optimal decision rules vary systematically with changes in the structure of series relevant to the decision maker, it follows that any change in policy will systematically alter the structure of econometric models.

Strip the register and it says: **the regularity you fitted was people's behaviour under the old policy. Change the policy and you change the behaviour, which means you change the regularity you were relying on to choose the policy.**

That is not measurement corrupting a fact. It is subtler and worse. The curve was never quite the thing it appeared to be — it was missing a term, expectations, that had been dormant across the entire sample because nobody had been leaning on it. **Acting on it is what made the missing term visible.** The lever did not break the fact. The lever revealed that the fact had been describing a world in which no lever existed.

And this is where I have to stop writing about economists.

I have spent two years compressing my own practice into instruments. Gates, ladders, cards, a set of tests, a homepage with six rungs on it that I derived by writing seven essays and noticing what they had in common. That last one is a real compression: seven pieces of thinking, held in six lines.

Then I put the six lines on the front of the site, where they are the navigation.

Which means the compression became the controller. And a fortnight ago I retired the seven essays it was fitted to, wrote three new ones, and found — this week, out loud, in a conversation I can go back and read — that I could no longer say cleanly which essay stood behind which rung. I treated that as a bookkeeping problem.

It is not a bookkeeping problem. **A ladder you navigate by starts choosing what you write next**, because it is easier to write the essay that presses a rung than the one that has nowhere to sit. Every essay I have produced since the ladder went up sits comfortably on it. That is not evidence the ladder is right. It is what a fitted model does to the data it subsequently generates.

There is one rung with no essay behind it. I read that as a gap in my output. It is at least as likely to be a gap the ladder produced — the one place my own compression cannot see, and therefore the one place I never went.

The correction is not to stop compressing. Compression is the only reason I can hold any of this at all, and Phillips's curve was a genuine achievement that predicted decades it had never seen. It is to know which of my instruments were fitted to a period when I was not yet using them, and to treat every one of those as **owing a re-derivation the moment it becomes something I steer by.**

Samuelson and Solow printed the warning. Friedman published the mechanism six years early. Goodhart wrote the law down as a joke in 1975, and Campbell wrote it independently a year later, and Lucas proved the general case the year after that, and the aggregate still overshot by a hundred per cent.

Knowing this in advance has never once been the difficulty.

Phillips fitted his curve to ninety-six years in which nobody was pulling on it.
