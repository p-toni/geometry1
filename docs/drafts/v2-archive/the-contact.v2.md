## Three passes

In the summer of 1628, before the warship *Vasa* left Stockholm, her captain arranged a demonstration.

Thirty men ran back and forth across the upper deck, in step, to set her rolling. It is the only stability test the seventeenth century had: no instruments, no arithmetic, just weight moved deliberately from side to side to see what the hull would do about it.

Vice-Admiral Klas Fleming stood and watched.

He stopped it after **three passes.**

Three. Not thirty. The ship was heeling so far, at anchor, in still water, with thirty men aboard instead of four hundred and fifty, that the officer supervising the test ended it because he believed she would go over in the harbour.

I want to be precise about what happened there, because the ordinary reading is wrong. Nobody failed to notice. Nobody was fooled. The probe was designed correctly, run correctly, and read correctly by a competent man who stopped it at exactly the right moment for exactly the right reason.

The world answered. It answered immediately, unambiguously, and in front of witnesses.

## He wished the king were at home

Then Fleming let her sail.

According to the ship's master, what he said as he walked away was that **he wished the king were at home.**

Gustavus Adolphus had ordered the ship, approved her specifications, and was at that moment on campaign in Poland, sending letters demanding to know why she was not yet at sea. There was no procedure by which a vice-admiral's afternoon on a rolling deck could be converted into a decision to stop. The result had nowhere to go.

This is the part I got wrong for a year, and it is the reason my own gates kept failing while I was still running them.

I had been treating contact as a *test*. Build the map, then go and touch something, and let the touching tell me whether the map holds. That is right as far as it goes, and it does not go far enough, because a test produces a result and a result is not yet anything.

> [thesis|the channel]
> A test tells you what is true. A **channel** is what makes the answer land somewhere that can act on it. Fleming had the first and not the second, and the first alone is worth exactly nothing.

[fig|channel-break]
*The test ran, returned, and was read correctly. The answer had nowhere to arrive, so the map was never revised.*

So the question is not *did I check.* I check constantly. The question has a destination in it.

**Where does the error get integrated?** Where does responsibility land, where does memory update, where does taste push back, where does the loop actually learn? If I cannot name the place, I do not have a learning loop. I have a man on a deck, counting passes, wishing somebody else were in the room.

## 1,300 metres

She sailed on 10 August 1628.

Four sails set. A salute fired. A crowd along the shore, and foreign ambassadors watching, because this was the most expensive object the Swedish crown had ever built and the point of her was partly to be seen.

A gust came off the bluffs. She heeled, righted, took a second gust, heeled further, and did not come back. Water came in through the open lower gunports. She went down in about thirty-two metres of water, roughly a hundred and twenty metres from shore.

**She had sailed about 1,300 metres.** Around thirty people died.

Two years of construction. The best shipwrights available. A king's attention on every detail. All of that coherence, accumulated and inspected and approved from the inside — and twenty minutes of actual water disposed of the entire question.

That asymmetry is the whole argument for contact and I do not think it can be argued down. A map can be internally consistent for as long as you like. Consistency is cheap; it is the one property a wrong map is most likely to have, because a wrong map has never been disturbed. The Vasa was not sloppy. She was *immaculate* — carved, painted, gilded, admired — and immaculate is a property of the interior.

A map cannot certify itself from the inside.

## No calculation existed

Here is what stops this being a story about people ignoring the evidence.

In the 1620s there was **no mathematical theory of ship stability**. None. There was no metacentric height to compute, no formula to check the design against, no calculation anybody could have run and got wrong. The Dutch method was shell-first: build by eye and by rule of thumb, from proportions that had worked before.

So there was no interior gate available. Not a weak one — *none.*

Which means the thirty men on the deck were not a supplementary check on a design that had already been verified some better way. They were the **only** verification that existed. The entire epistemic apparatus available for the question *will this ship stay upright* was: put weight on it and look.

They ran it. It failed. She sailed.

I find this clarifying about my own instruments, because it names their limit precisely. My gates — can I rebuild this with the source closed, does the map survive weakening one edge, do I know what I cut away — are all **interior**. Every one of them tests whether I can carry the structure. Not one of them tests whether the structure meets anything.

That is not a flaw in them. It is their type. An interior gate is the right instrument for an ownership claim and the wrong instrument for a bet on the world, and the failure I keep making is not skipping a gate but reaching for the wrong class of gate and feeling, afterwards, as though I had checked.

And there is a worse case than a wrong map, which took me too long to name:

The bad map is not always wrong. Sometimes it is **numb.** It has been arranged so that nothing can come back to it — no prediction it could miss, no probe that could return badly, no reader who will fail to nod. A numb map cannot be refuted because it has declined to say anything a refutation could attach to. It ages very well and teaches nothing.

Self-sealed maps age into personality. Answerable maps age into practice.

## The gunports were open

One more detail, because it is the one I keep returning to as a person who builds interfaces.

The lower gunports were open when she sailed, and they were open for a good reason: a warship leaving harbour under salute has her guns run out. That is what the ports are *for*. The ship was doing the thing the ship was designed to do.

An edge — the height of a gunport sill above the waterline — is not only a boundary. It is a **promise about what will happen when you press it.** That sill said: *heel this far and the sea stays out.* And it was true, for the range of heel anybody had reason to expect.

The Vasa heeled past it. The affordance held its promise right up to the edge of a condition nobody had verified, and past that point it did the opposite of what it advertised — it let the sea into the hull through a hole the design had put there on purpose.

That is the failure mode I most fear in what I build. Not the control that plainly does not work; that gets fixed on the first press. The one that answers correctly across every case anyone tested, and inverts outside it, silently, while continuing to look exactly as it did.

## Nobody was found guilty

The inquiry called witnesses, examined the shipwrights, and found no one to blame. The king had approved the specifications. The designer had died before she was launched. There was, in the end, nobody who could have been the channel, and so the finding was that there was no fault.

I do not think that verdict was a cover-up. I think it was accurate, and that its accuracy is the most uncomfortable thing here. The system had a test, competent people, an unambiguous result, and no place for the result to land — and a system like that has no guilty party. It has an architecture.

So the work is not to check more. It is to build somewhere for an answer to arrive — named before the probe runs, not looked for after it comes back.

That is the step I have always left implicit, and it is the only one the Vasa failed.

Everything else that day went right.

> [update|provenance]
> *Vasa* detail from the Vasa Museum's account and the standard record: the lurching test of thirty men halted by Vice-Admiral Klas Fleming after three passes; the maiden voyage of 10 August 1628, approximately 1,300 metres, roughly thirty dead, sunk in about 32 m of water some 120 m from shore with the lower gunports open; the inquiry finding no guilty party; and the absence of any mathematical stability theory in the 1620s. Fleming's remark that he wished the king were at home is reported by the ship's master.

The ship was raised in 1961 and stands in a museum in Stockholm, largely intact, because the water she sank in was too cold and too brackish for shipworm. You can go and look at her. She is there because she failed at 1,300 metres instead of in the North Sea — early enough to be recovered whole.

Fleming was right on the deck that afternoon, and being right was the whole of what he got.
